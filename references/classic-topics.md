# Vintage topic development

## Extract the reference model

Read the supplied distilled summary, transcript, or reference material deeply. Before proposing topics, briefly explain the following in Chinese:

- Topic type: for example, a quiet daily refuge, slow wandering, or a low-pressure care space.
- Story architecture: how entry, settling, sustained attention, and fading attention develop.
- Presentation style: pacing, social pressure, plot intensity, use of characters, and narration posture.
- Sensory formula: how sound, light, smell, touch, and temperature support the experience; note the level of detail.
- Sleep value: how this particular experience lets the listener disengage from tasks and repetitive thinking.

Inherit the reference's safety and downshift mechanism, not its surface props. A flower-market reference does not make glass walls, canals, flowers, or warm lamps mandatory. These details are evidence of the mechanism, not a checklist of ingredients.

When no reference is supplied, develop topics from the user's direction and audience guidance. State that there is no reference model rather than inventing source attribution.

## Test whether each setting works

Before presenting a candidate, answer:

1. What is this place, and is it real or a believable quiet adaptation of a real place?
2. Why is it quiet at this time? Rain, early morning, an off-season interval, or permitted after-hours access can work; nighttime is not mandatory.
3. Why may the listener enter and remain without explaining themselves, buying, working, hurrying, performing, or responding socially?
4. Why do the objects, sounds, access route, and environmental details belong together?
5. What makes the place feel safe, and what sustained experience can support approximately thirty minutes of narration?

Replace candidates that only assemble unrelated soothing elements. Do not derive ten topics from ten corners of the same reference setting. Keep the inherited mechanism recognizable while varying the setting, object system, and memorable motif.

Vintage experience is grounded in concrete spaces, objects, and environmental details. If its core depends on an original world's impossible law, ask whether the user wants to switch to secondary-world fantasy; the old general label "self-consistent fantasy" does not bypass the new category workflow.

## Present ten comparable candidate cards

Use the same fields across candidates so the operator can compare them:

| Field | Language | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Numeral | 1–10 |
| Title | English | A concise working title |
| Hook | English | One sentence conveying the experience and mood |
| Setting credibility | Chinese | Real setting or believable adaptation, and why it works |
| Setting and experience | Chinese | Space, time, conditions, and a slow stay or wandering route |
| Sensory palette | Chinese | The principal sound, light, smell, touch, and temperature anchors |
| Audience fit and safety | Chinese | Why this experience suits the relevant sleep context without pressure |
| Reference inheritance | Chinese | The reference mechanism being retained; omit across all cards if there is no reference |
| Differentiation | Chinese | How the setting, objects, and memorable motif differ from the reference and other candidates |

Do not restore an old rigid title-length quota or unsupported demographic assumptions. Make the cards substantive but concise enough to compare.

Stop after the candidates and wait for selection. If the user explicitly supplies an already confirmed complete theme and requests writing, proceed without regenerating ten topics.

## Carry the selection into writing

Use every substantive field of the selected card, along with the reference-model analysis and any user revisions, in the working brief described in `../SKILL.md`. Preserve its differentiation, sensory detail density, access logic, and safety mechanism through Parts 2–4. Do not reduce the selected card to its title and then write a generic scene.
